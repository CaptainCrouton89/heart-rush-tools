# Timescale

This page helps you choose how much of the game to play out and how much to roll, and how to run it when a roll covers days, weeks or seasons. The rules for those rolls are in the Stratagem System section. The player-facing description of the scales is in How To Play, under Scales of Play.

## Two Ways to Resolve Anything

You can play something out, or you can roll it. Playing it out means the table acts through it in scenes, using the normal rules for skill checks, combat and conversation. Rolling it means one check stands in for a stretch of time.

A stratagem roll stands for a stretch of time toward a goal too big for one check. One check that settles a task is a skill check, whatever time it covers.

Each stratagem roll covers a set time, and the Stratagem System section governs it. Between rolls you play out the parts the table cares about, and what happens in those scenes changes the next roll.

## Choosing the Scale

Let the fiction and the table's energy decide. If the players lean forward and ask specific questions, play it out. If they check their phones or say "we just want to get there," roll it.

**Play it out when:**

- The stakes are immediate and personal.
- The players want direct control over the outcome.
- One action could change the situation dramatically.
- The scene matters to the story.
- It is a chase: a chase is never a stratagem and has its own rules (see Chase). A fight is played out unless the party clearly wins it, in which case Quick Combat (Misc. Other Rules) can roll it.

**Roll it when:**

- The outcome matters more than the process.
- Time has to pass without bogging down play.
- The players show no interest in the details.
- The activity is routine or repetitive.
- You need to summarize to reach the scenes that matter.

Any player can ask for either. A player may also play any objective of a stratagem as a scene instead of rolling it, so never refuse that request because a roll would be faster. If nothing is at risk and nothing is uncertain, you do not need a roll at all: narrate it.

## Switching at the Table

You can change scale at any point, and it needs no announcement beyond what you say aloud.

- **To roll it:** Summarize and set the next stretch of time. _"The battle is over and you have made it back to camp. You will be here three weeks while the army regroups. What do you want to get done?"_ If the answer is a goal with several objectives, such as getting the quartermaster's office to supply the whole company (win the quartermaster, find the missing stores, and get the captain's order), it becomes a stratagem with a roll per week.
- **To play it out:** Stop summarizing and start a scene. _"Three weeks in, you find a page missing from the archive ledger, and the clerk is standing at your elbow..."_
- **To let the players choose:** Ask. _"Do you want to play out the negotiation, or tell me your approach and roll it?"_

## Running a Stratagem

This is guidance for the GM. The rules are in the Stratagem System section.

### Choose the Time per Roll

Choose the time per roll from the goal: hours for a rescue before dawn, a week for a siege, a season for a trade route. A shorter time means more rolls and more room for scenes between them. A longer time means fewer rolls and more is summarized.

Pick a time that is long enough that nobody wants to play it moment by moment, and short enough that the situation can change between rolls.

If there is a deadline, the stratagem ends when it passes and the event it guards happens with whatever the players have finished; unfinished objectives are not finished. When an objective is itself a stratagem, it takes as long as its inner stratagem took, not an extra outer period, and other leaders keep rolling on the outer schedule meanwhile.

### Before the Roll

Say the CN, as for every check. Say any consequence you have attached to success, so the players can choose a different objective first, and say the risk a player's choice turns on if there is one, such as cheap shipping against secure shipping.

Do not say what failure will cost. Decide that when the roll fails, from what has happened in the fiction.

### When a Roll Fails

A failed roll always costs the time it covered, and usually more, so make the time matter: the siege goes on, the season is gone, the dawn is closer. Sometimes the time is the whole cost. The objective stays open and the players can try again.

A scene after a failed roll, whether you offer it from a worse position or the players start it, spends no further period unless it carries on into the next objective. If it goes badly the objective stays open.

Any cost can come from one roll, but when the roll stood in for something the characters do in person, a worse starting position for a scene is usually the better cost. _"You try to slip out and the guards catch you on the wall; combat begins"_ gives the players a scene to play. _"You are caught and jailed"_ from one roll takes that scene away.

### When a Die Shows a 1

The player chooses between failing and rerolling that die and taking a complication. On a roll that covers a week or a season, make the complication belong to that stretch of time, not to one moment in it. The players may play it out as a scene right away; if they do not, record it as a hindrance and move on.

Players are likelier to want a scene when the complication holds a decision they would want to make. When it is a lasting problem and a scene would only repeat what you have already said, they are likelier to want it recorded.

### Scenes Between Rolls

A scene played instead of the roll takes the period the roll would have, faces the same situation, and finishes the objective if what happened in it would plausibly finish it; a scene after a failed roll can finish it too. An intervention makes the roll easier and never finishes the objective. Its time is added before the period, so the roll's period starts after the scene, and under a deadline that time comes off the clock.

When a scene changes the odds, size the effect from how the scene actually went. If it helps only the next roll, give a modifier: ±3 for a small gain, ±6 for a major one, ±9 for a legendary one. If the change will still matter after the next roll, name it and record it as an asset or hindrance.

A scene can also go badly and hurt, so a botched attempt can give a hindrance as readily as a good one gives an asset. An asset or hindrance applies to every later roll it bears on, at any level of nested stratagems, and is never added twice to one roll. When a scene is played instead of the roll, name the concrete fact each asset and hindrance becomes, and set its checks from the situation.

### When It Becomes a Fight

If the players choose to play out the fight, or the fiction demands it, stop rolling and run the fight with the combat rules. Turn each asset and hindrance the stratagem has earned into a concrete condition of the fight, such as surprise, difficult terrain, reinforcements on a stated round, or militia fleeing when the first one falls. The Stratagem System section has worked examples.

If the party will clearly win and nobody wants to play it, the Quick Combat rule settles it with rolls. If you would not bet on the party, play the fight out.

## Rules That Set a Stratagem Up for You

Several sections fix the setup in advance, so you do not have to choose it. Each is still a stratagem: scenes, assets and hindrances work as the Stratagem System section describes.

- **Journeys:** each segment is an objective, the terrain table gives the CN, and the failure list gives the costs.
- **Trade Routes & Merchants:** one roll per season that takes its leader's period like any objective, the goods and shipping choice set the CN, and a table gives the failure cost. A repeating operation may record one lasting change it causes as a single asset, once.
- **Strongholds** (Building Against Obstacles): 2 to 3 objectives at a fixed CN, different leaders may pursue different objectives in the same quarter of the build time, each objective at most one roll per quarter, a failure costs time, a share of the money, or both.
- **Magical Crafting:** each level of the item is an objective, the item's level sets the time per roll and the CN, and the failure costs are listed there.
- **Quick Combat** (Misc. Other Rules): four outcomes as objectives, the opponents set the CN, and each outcome has its own failure result.
- **Learning a Language** (Misc. Other Rules): four milestones in order, the language sets the time per roll, the way you learn sets the CN, and a failure costs only the time.
- **Missions** (Building Your Character): at least 3 objectives, counted at the level where the mission was set up, and a minimum setup CN from your Heart die and profession rank, with real stakes.

## Quick Reference

**Play it out:**

- Chases, and any fight the party does not clearly win
- Tense negotiations
- Stealth and trap sequences
- Dramatic confrontations
- Any scene the players want to play

**Roll it:**

- Travel without incidents
- Research and investigation
- Social gatherings
- Crafting and downtime
- Routine preparations
- Any goal the players want to summarize

**A stratagem roll stands for a stretch of time toward a goal too big for one check.** Set the time per roll, say the CN, and say any consequence of success. Decide what a failure costs when it happens.

**When uncertain:**

- Ask the players.
- Consider how much the scene matters to the story.
- Follow the table's engagement.
- Think about pacing.
